## Project Overview

Syntactic Bootstrapping of Mental State Verbs in Mandarin-Speaking Children examines whether children can use syntax to infer the meaning of novel mental-state verbs, and whether that learning relates to theory-of-mind and complement-clause understanding.

<div class="project-authors">Authors: Yuanyuan Chang<sup>1</sup>, Yuexin Li<sup>2</sup>, Sining Gao<sup>1</sup>, Tianyi Liu<sup>1</sup>, Haiwei Liu<sup>1</sup>, Peter Gordon<sup>1</sup></div>
<div class="project-affiliations"><sup>1</sup> Teachers College, Columbia University<br><sup>2</sup> Columbia University</div>

## Background

- Syntactic bootstrapping proposes that children can infer verb meaning from grammatical structure, even for abstract mental-state meanings.
- In Mandarin, explicit morpho-syntactic marking is less overt, making this a strong test case for structure-based learning.
- Desire and belief verbs differ in typical argument realization and complement usage, which may provide distributional cues in child-directed input.
- Prior work suggests complement-clause mastery may relate to theory-of-mind development, but findings are mixed across studies and languages.

## Research Questions

- Can Mandarin-speaking children use syntactic information to infer meanings of a novel mental-state verb?
- Does complement-clause comprehension support this learning?
- Do vocabulary/comprehension measures for mental-state language relate to false-belief performance?

## Method

- Participants: `30` Mandarin-speaking children (mean age about `51` months), with balanced gender distribution.
- Training phase:
  - Each child heard a story where a pseudoword appeared repeatedly (`12` times).
  - Three syntax conditions were used:
    - Desire group: desire-type syntax
    - Belief group: belief-type syntax
    - Mixture group: alternating desire-type and belief-type syntax
  - Comprehension checks ensured children tracked story meaning.
- Testing phase:
  - Syntax test prompt: “Which sentence sounds better?”
  - Semantics test with two short clips (belief scenario vs. desire scenario), followed by puppet-comment interpretation.
- Additional assessments:
  - Mental-state vocabulary from narrative elicitation
  - False-belief understanding tasks
  - Complement comprehension task (true and false complements)

## Results Summary

- Hypothesis 1 (syntactic bootstrapping) was supported:
  - Belief group showed `58%` belief responses in the syntax test and `71%` in the semantics test.
  - Desire group showed `54%` desire responses in syntax and `62%` in semantics.
  - Mixture group showed `64%` belief responses in syntax and `63%` in semantics.
- Group differences were significant in semantics (`F(2,22)=5.93`, `p<0.01`) but not syntax (`F(2,24)=0.99`, `p=0.38`).
- Post-hoc contrasts showed the mixture and belief groups differed significantly from the desire group in semantic responses.
- For hypothesis 2, direct links between theory-of-mind performance and complement/mental-state vocabulary measures were not significant.
- Age predicted false-belief performance (`r(25)=0.43`, `p=0.03`).
- Complement comprehension significantly predicted syntax and semantics test performance in regression models.

## Discussion

- Children used syntactic structure to learn a novel mental-state verb in Mandarin, consistent with syntactic bootstrapping accounts.
- When syntax cues were mixed, children still leaned toward belief-like interpretations, suggesting a belief-default tendency under ambiguity.
- Complement-clause comprehension related to task performance, but evidence for a direct complement-theory-of-mind link remained weak in this sample.
- Overall, results support syntax-driven verb-meaning learning while highlighting limits in linking this process to broader theory-of-mind outcomes.
